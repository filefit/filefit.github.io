What makes a PDF large — and how to shrink it without ruining it

Two PDFs with the same number of pages can differ in size by a factor of a hundred. Knowing why helps you choose the right way to make yours smaller.

What takes up space inside a PDF

  • Images — by far the biggest part. A scanned page is a photo of paper; at high resolution one page can be several MB.
  • Embedded fonts — usually tens of KB each; small compared to scans.
  • Text and drawings — tiny. A 20-page text-only PDF is often under 100 KB.
  • Overhead — duplicated objects and unused data left behind by editing tools.

Two ways to make a PDF smaller

1. Lossless optimisation

The file is rewritten more efficiently: objects are packed together and unused data is dropped. Nothing visible changes and text stays selectable. This often saves a little on PDFs that were edited several times, but it cannot shrink large scanned images much.

2. Re-rendering pages as images

Each page is drawn at a chosen resolution (dots per inch) and saved as a compressed image. This can shrink a 5 MB scan to under 200 KB. The trade-off: text becomes part of the picture, so it can no longer be selected or searched, and very low resolutions make small print hard to read.

FileFit always tries the lossless method first. Only if the file is still above your limit does it re-render pages, starting at a high resolution and stepping down until the file fits — so you get the sharpest result the limit allows, and it tells you which method it used.

Tips for small, readable PDFs

  • Black & white is ideal for scanned text documents: colour information is dropped, often halving the size with no loss of readability.
  • Split long documents if a portal asks for one file per document — fewer pages per file means better quality per page.
  • Scan at a sensible resolution: 150–200 dpi is plenty for most documents; 600 dpi scans are huge and rarely needed.
  • Zoom in and check the smallest print after compressing, especially with very low limits like 100 KB for many pages.
